Dr. Shneider is a physician-scientist whose laboratory works at the intersection of basic motor neuron biology and clinical neuromuscular disease. He trained in the laboratories of Richard Axel and Thomas Jessell before returning to Columbia, where he directs the Eleanor and Lou Gehrig ALS Center and treats patients with ALS and related motor neuron disorders.

The lab uses mouse genetics and patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) models to study how mutant RNA-binding proteins — including FUS and TDP-43 — form abnormal ribonucleoprotein assemblies that are selectively toxic to motor neurons. The long-term goal is to turn mechanistic insight into genetic and molecular therapies that reach patients.

Translating mechanism into medicine

Silencing toxic FUS with antisense oligonucleotides

In FUS-ALS, mutant FUS protein accumulates in motor neurons and becomes toxic. The lab helped develop ulefnersen (ION363, originally named jacifusen), an antisense oligonucleotide that binds FUS pre-mRNA and directs its degradation — lowering production of the toxic protein before it can harm the cell.

This approach was first validated in a FUS knock-in mouse model, then in a single patient treated under compassionate use, and most recently in a 12-patient case series. It is now being evaluated in a global clinical trial through the lab's Silence ALS program.
