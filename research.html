Research

We study why motor neurons are selectively vulnerable in ALS — from the molecular behavior of disease-associated RNA-binding proteins, to patient-derived cellular models, to genetic strategies aimed at slowing disease in patients.

01

RNA-binding protein toxicity

Mutations in RNA-binding proteins such as FUS and TDP-43 cause inherited forms of ALS, and abnormal forms of these proteins are found in the majority of sporadic cases as well. We study how mutant FUS and TDP-43 mislocalize and form abnormal ribonucleoprotein assemblies, and why this toxic gain of function selectively injures motor neurons rather than other cell types.

Mouse genetics RNA biology Protein aggregation Electrophysiology
02

Patient-derived disease models

We generate and characterize induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) lines from clinically well-characterized ALS patients, differentiating them into motor neurons to study disease mechanisms directly in human cells. These models let us connect a patient's genetics and clinical course to cellular phenotypes we can measure and manipulate in the lab.

iPSC differentiation Live-cell imaging Patient cohorts
03

Toward genetic therapies

Using animal and cellular models of motor neuron degeneration, we identify candidate targets and evaluate genetic and molecular strategies — including approaches that reduce or correct toxic protein expression — with the goal of translating findings from the bench toward treatments for ALS and related motor neuron diseases.

Target validation Preclinical models Translational neuroscience
